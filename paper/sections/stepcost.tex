\section{What sets the cost of a step}
\label{sec:stepcost}

\paragraph{Step size.}
The step log confirmed that the budget was applied exactly: no step scheduled more
than $B$ tokens, and each run's logged prompt tokens equalled the number sent.
Milestone 5's primary prediction was that a step's cost tracks its scheduled tokens,
measured by the interval from one scheduling call to the next. Pooled over the seven
C32 series, steps of at least 1024 tokens were followed by a median interval of 56\ms,
against 5.7\ms{} for steps of at most 64 tokens (9.8$\times$, Spearman 0.87), and the
prediction passed. At C8 the ratio was 9.0$\times$ but the Spearman correlation was
0.57, below its threshold of 0.7, which under the protocol's rule rejects the
hypothesis's first clause at C8.

\paragraph{A lagged measure.}
vLLM 0.29.0 can schedule step $n{+}1$ before it processes step $n$'s results, so the
interval credited to step $n$ partly reflects the cost of step $n{-}1$. In the C8
block the engine scheduled 97--100\% of steps this way, and at $B{=}2048$ a step's
interval correlated 0.96--0.99 with the previous step's execution time, against
0.69--0.82 with its own (post hoc; Figure~\ref{fig:timing}). Some intervals are too
short to be a step's own cost: at C8 with $B{=}8192$ and $L{=}1024$, steps of at least
1024 tokens had a median interval of 16.5\ms, while a single 1024-token chunk took a
median of at least 19.5\ms{} at every context we measured. Part~2's pre-registered
check found that execution time tracked a step's tokens better than the interval did
at C8 (Spearman 0.63 against 0.57), though still below the threshold. By execution
time, large steps took 6.6--29.6$\times$ as long as small ones, with per-series Spearman
correlations of 0.63--0.89 (post hoc). No C32 run recorded execution time. Taking each
C32 step's cost from the next step's interval, which matches C8 execution time within
3\% for large steps, gives 9.9$\times$ and a Spearman correlation of 0.92. The
pre-registered verdicts stand.

\paragraph{Packing (post hoc).}
At $B{=}2048$ and $L{=}1024$, 94\% of steps carrying at least 1024 prompt tokens held
two or more prompts, and the median prompt-carrying step was a full 2048 tokens.
Because the scheduler fills the budget with waiting prompts, a shorter prompt changes
how often steps carry prompt work (38\% of steps at $L{=}1024$, against 77\% at
$L{=}2048$) more than how large those steps are. The median therefore fell far (6.1
against 55\ms) while the tail fell only 19\%. Our workload favors packing: all
requests have the same lengths and start together, so they finish and return in
groups.

\paragraph{Context.}
The pilot had suggested a role for context, because 2048-token steps were followed by
shorter intervals at shorter prompts (45\ms{} at $L{=}1024$, 55.5\ms{} at $L{=}2048$).
We withdraw that reading: at both lengths those steps start at context 0, and the gap
follows the share of steps that carry prompt work (38\% against 77\%), as a lagged
measure would. A second pre-registered design tested context directly. One request at a
time (C1) sent 8192-token prompts at budgets 1024 and 2048, so every prompt step held
one chunk of exactly $B$ tokens at a known context position, timed by execution time
(Figure~\ref{fig:context}). Chunk cost rose almost linearly with context. 1024-token
chunks took 25.0\ms{} at context 0, 19.5\ms{} at context 1024, and 35.9\ms{} at
context 7168; 2048-token chunks rose from 41.7\ms{} at context 0 to 67.4\ms{} at
context 6144. The deepest chunk cost 1.44$\times$ and 1.62$\times$ the first
(Spearman 0.96 in both series). From context 2048 on, each additional 1024 tokens of
context added about 1.9\ms{} to a 1024-token chunk and 3.8\ms{} to a 2048-token chunk.

These slopes match a FLOP count. A chunk of $n$ tokens at context $c$ spends about
$4ncd$ attention FLOPs per layer on the prior context, with $d{=}896$ and 24 layers, so
each 1024 tokens of context costs 0.09 TFLOP for a 1024-token chunk and 0.18 TFLOP for
a 2048-token chunk. That is 1.2 and 2.4\ms{} at the tensor peak, and 2.3 and 4.6\ms{}
at the 40 TFLOPS that the context-0 2048-token chunk achieved. The measured slopes lie
between the two and double with chunk size. The first chunk of each prompt was slower
than the second at $B{=}1024$ (25.0 against 19.5\ms), and we do not know why;
per-request setup does not explain it, because at C8 a lone 1024-token chunk at
context 0 took 16.2\ms. The slow first chunk lowers ratios measured from context 0:
taken from the second chunk, the $B{=}1024$ ratio is 1.84$\times$, and a 2048-token
chunk at context 0 cost 1.67$\times$ a 1024-token chunk, where FLOPs predict about
2.1$\times$.

\paragraph{A step-cost model (post hoc).}
We fit execution time as a fixed cost plus terms for scheduled tokens, the attention
pairs of prompt chunks, $\sum n(c + n/2)$, and the context of decoding requests. Over
all 30{,}789 Part~2 steps the fit has $R^2 = 0.993$, with linear and attention work at
43 and 42 TFLOPS. Fit only on the one-request context series, the model predicts the
ITL p99 of all six C8 series within 5\%, weighting each step by the decoding requests
it carries. It overpredicts the C8 median at long prompts, because one-request data
cannot identify the decode term. Execution time itself, weighted the same way,
reproduces the client's ITL p50 within 2\% and p99 within 6\%.
