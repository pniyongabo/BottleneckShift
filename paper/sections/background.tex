\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

A request's life in vLLM has three phases: it waits in a \emph{queue}, its prompt is
processed (\emph{prefill}), and its output is generated one token per engine step
(\emph{decode}). Iteration-level scheduling admits and retires requests between steps
\citep{yu2022orca}, and paged KV-cache management keeps memory for many concurrent
requests \citep{kwon2023vllm}. With \emph{chunked prefill}, a long prompt is split
across several steps and shares each step with the running decodes
\citep{agrawal2024sarathiserve}. The \emph{token budget}
(\texttt{max\_num\_batched\_tokens}) caps the tokens one step may process, decodes
plus prompt chunks. vLLM's documentation describes the trade: smaller budgets give
better inter-token latency, and larger ones better time to first token and throughput
\citep{vllmdocsoptimization}. Our results refine this: at high concurrency, a larger
budget improved median inter-token latency and worsened mean time to first token
(Section~\ref{sec:budget}).

We use the client-side metrics of vLLM's benchmark. TTFT is the time from sending a
request to receiving its first token. Inter-token latency (ITL) is the gap between
consecutive streamed tokens of one request. E2E is the total request latency. The
\emph{share of latency before the first token} is $\sum \mathrm{TTFT} / \sum
\mathrm{E2E}$ over a run's requests. We report ITL percentiles because averages hide
jitter \citep{agrawal2024etalon}: a median and a 99th percentile can rank
configurations differently, and tails matter at scale \citep{dean2013tail}. The
benchmark pools the gaps of all requests in a run, and one engine step produces a gap
for every request it carries, so a run's ITL p99 reflects its slowest few steps.
