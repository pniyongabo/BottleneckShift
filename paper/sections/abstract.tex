\begin{abstract}
When an LLM inference server slows down under load, the added latency can come from
waiting in the queue, processing the prompt, or generating each token, and the split
can change with load. This paper aims to establish which phase dominates as load grows
and what determines how long each step takes. We measured vLLM serving a
0.5B-parameter model on one GPU in a series of pre-registered experiments totaling
18{,}900 measured requests and \$2.59 of GPU time. Most runs were observed from the
client, the server's phase metrics, and GPU and CPU telemetry, and the last
experiments added a per-step scheduler log. Every prediction was published before its
data and scored after; of 59 scored predictions, 40 held, and we report the failures
in full.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item From 8 to 32 concurrent requests, past throughput saturation, the added
    latency moves into each engine step (scheduler iteration). The share of latency
    before the first token falls from 0.44 to 0.24 while median inter-token latency
    rises from 4.9 to 55\,ms.
  \item The scheduler's token budget decides where that delay appears. At 32
    concurrent requests, a larger budget gives a 7\,ms median and 18\% more
    throughput but a 169\,ms 99th percentile, and with the smallest budget queueing
    takes two thirds of each request's latency.
  \item With one request at a time, a fixed-size prompt chunk costs up to
    1.6$\times$ more at deeper context, in line with a count of attention FLOPs, and
    a cost model fit on those data predicts the 99th-percentile inter-token latency
    at 8 concurrent requests within 5\% (post hoc). The pre-registered test that a
    step's cost tracks its scheduled tokens failed at 8 concurrent requests and
    passed at 32 with a timing measure we later found lags by one step.
\end{enumerate}

Under load, then, latency depends on how the scheduler splits prompt work into steps
and on how long each step takes, as well as on how many requests are running. A median
alone can hide the tail: the budget with the best median had the worst 99th
percentile. Client and GPU metrics showed the added latency, and the per-step log
traced it to steps that carry prompt work.
\end{abstract}
