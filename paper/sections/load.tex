\section{Where latency goes under load}
\label{sec:load}

\paragraph{Decode dominates each request, even with long prompts.}
We first compared a prefill-heavy workload (2048 input and 32 output tokens) with a
decode-heavy one (64 and 512) at C1 and C8. We had predicted that time before the first
token would dominate the prefill-heavy workload, but a request running alone spent
about 40\ms{} in prefill and about 122\ms{} in its 31 generation steps. A cost estimate
predicts this \citep{pope2023scaling}. Prefill of 2048 tokens needs about 1.6 TFLOP
(two FLOPs per weight per token, plus attention), or 21\ms{} at the A4000's dense
16-bit tensor peak of 77 TFLOPS. Each generation step must read about 1\,GB of
weights, at least 2.2\ms{} at 448\,GB/s, or 68\ms{} for 31 steps
\citep{nvidiaa4000}. Even at these ideal rates prefill is about a quarter of the
request, so the prediction could have been made in advance.

From C1 to C8 the share of latency before the first token rose from 31\% to 45\%. (At
C1 the client's 54\ms{} TTFT includes about 14\ms{} of request handling outside the
server's prefill time.) The decode-heavy workload gained 6.46$\times$ throughput for
10\% slower per-token time; the prefill-heavy one gained only 2.8$\times$, with
queueing and a tenfold rise in ITL p99. At C8 much of a request's decode time is spent
in steps that also carry other requests' prompt chunks, so a per-request phase
breakdown books GPU prefill work as decode time. In Milestone 5's step log, 23\% of C8
steps at this prompt length carried prompt work.

\paragraph{Past saturation, the added latency moves into each step.}
Raising concurrency to C16 and C32, we predicted that time before the first token would
come to dominate. Instead, its share fell from 0.44 at C8 to 0.35 at C16 and 0.24 at
C32. Output throughput peaked at C16 (583 tokens/s) and fell slightly at C32 (555),
and median ITL rose from 4.9\ms{} at C8 and 5.5\ms{} at C16 to 55\ms{} at C32
(Figure~\ref{fig:crossover}). A pre-registered sweep (C8, C12, C16, C24, C32) on a
different machine reproduced these values within about 1\% and placed the jump between
C16 (5.5\ms) and C24 (45.8\ms). The jump is abrupt because the gaps form two clusters,
below 10\ms{} from decode-only steps and 40--80\ms{} from prompt-carrying steps. The
share of gaps above 20\ms{} rose from 31\% at C16 to 56\% at C24, while mean ITL rose
steadily: 8.7, 13.3, 18.0, 30.5, and 45.1\ms{} from C8 to C32 (post hoc).

\paragraph{The shift is bounded.}
At $B{=}2048$ a step costs at most about 56\ms{} (ITL p99 was 58\ms{} at C32), so a
request's 31 generation steps take at most about 1.8\,s. Past saturation throughput no
longer grows, so by Little's law each added request in flight adds latency, and once
steps are full it must go into the wait for the first token. Over full runs, mean TTFT
doubled from C8 (215\ms) to C32 (431\ms), but most of that comes from the burst of
requests that starts each run. Without each run's first and last C requests, it rose
only from 212 to 255\ms, and ITL changed by about 1\% (post hoc). The per-step shift
therefore describes C16 to C32 at this budget. Beyond C32, and under open-loop load,
we expect delay to return to TTFT; we did not test this.
